# 12.7 Timber Framing (Post & Beam)

Timber framing is the ancient method of creating structures using heavy squared-off timbers carefully fitted and joined with mortise and tenon joints, secured by strong wooden pegs.

> [!TIP]
> **Longevity**: A well-built timber frame can last 500+ years. The weakness is usually the roof or foundation, not the frame itself.

## 1. The Core Joints

### Mortise and Tenon
The fundamental joint.
*   **Tenon**: The projecting end of a piece of wood.
*   **Mortise**: The hole cut into a timber to receive the tenon.
*   **Peg**: A slightly tapered oak dowel that locks them together.

**Rule of Thumb**: The tenon thickness should be 1/3 the thickness of the timber. For an 8x8 post, use a 2.5" thick tenon.

### Scarf Joint
Used to join two timbers end-to-end to create a longer beam.
*   **Halved Scarf**: Simple overlap, bolted.
*   **Bladed Scarf with Wedge**: Lock together with tension keys.

## 2. Tools Required (Non-Electric)
1.  **Framing Chisel**: Heavy duty, 1.5" or 2" wide.
2.  **Auger (Boring Machine)**: To drill out the bulk of the mortise waste.
3.  **Slick**: A giant chisel for paring flat surfaces.
4.  **Mallet**: Heavy wood or rawhide (2-3 lbs).
5.  **Crosscut Saw**: For cutting timbers to length.

## 3. Raising the Frame
1.  **Bent Assembly**: Assembling a cross-section (Post-Beam-Post) on the ground.
2.  **Raising**: Using ropes, pulleys, and community power to lift the bent vertical.
3.  **Connecting**: Installing the girts (horizontal connectors) between bents.

## 4. Wood Selection
*   **Green Wood**: Easier to cut, shrinks as it dries (joints pull tight).
*   **White Oak**: The gold standard. Strong, rot-resistant.
*   **Douglas Fir**: Excellent strength-to-weight ratio.
*   **Pine/Spruce**: Good for rafters and non-structural infill.

> [!WARNING]
> **Check**: When using green wood, **draw-bore** your peg holes. Offset the hole in the tenon slightly towards the shoulder so the peg pulls the joint tight as it is driven in.
