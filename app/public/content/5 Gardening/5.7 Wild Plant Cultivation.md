# 5.7 Wild Plant Cultivation

**Concept:** The forest and fields already grow food—why not bring the best producers into your garden or encourage them where they stand?

**This is the bridge between foraging and farming.**

---

## Why Cultivate Wild Plants?

1. **Zero seed cost** (you transplant what's already growing)
2. **Proven local adaptation** (these plants thrive in your exact climate)
3. **Low maintenance** (wild plants don't need coddling)
4. **Expand your food diversity** (beyond tomatoes and lettuce)
5. **Permaculture foundation** (perennials that

 produce year after year)

---

## The Best Candidates for Cultivation

### Criteria for Selection
✅ **Edible and nutritious**  
✅ **Easy to identify** (no toxic look-alikes)  
✅ **Transplants well** (perennials are best)  
✅ **Productive** (yields enough to justify space)

---

## Top 10 Wild Edibles to Cultivate

### 1. Ramps (Wild Leeks)

**Why:** Prized gourmet vegetable, high value, perennial.

**Transplanting:**
- Dig in early spring (before they emerge) or fall
- Take entire bulb + roots
- Plant in shady, rich soil (under trees is ideal)
- Space 4-6" apart

**Patience required:** Takes 5-7 years to establish a good patch from bulbs (faster if you transplant large clumps).

**Harvest:** Take 1 leaf per plant (sustainable), or dig 1/3 of bulbs every few years.

---

### 2. Asparagus

**Where to find:** Ditches, old homesteads, roadsides (escaped cultivated plants gone wild).

**Transplanting:**
- Dig roots (crowns) in fall or early spring
- Plant in trenches (6-8" deep, rich soil)
- Space 12-18" apart

**Patience:** Takes 2-3 years before first harvest.

**Yield:** 20-year lifespan, produces for 6-8 weeks every spring.

---

### 3. Stinging Nettle

**Why:** Highest protein of any green (see section 12.1).

**Transplanting:**
- Dig roots in fall (wear gloves!)
- Plant in moist, rich soil (partial shade)
- Space 12" apart

**Warning:** Nettle spreads aggressively. Plant in a contained area (raised bed with barriers) or you'll regret it.

**Harvest:** Cut tops in spring (before flowering) for greens.

---

### 4. Dandelion

**Why:** Already in your yard—why not cultivate the best ones?

**Selection:** Find dandelions with the largest, least bitter leaves.

**Method:**
- Dig entire taproot in fall
- Replant in garden bed
- Let go to seed, save seeds from the best plants

**Over 3-5 generations, you'll have "domesticated" dandelions with superior flavor.**

---

### 5. Elderberry

**Why:** Immune-boosting berries, beautiful shrub, attracts pollinators.

**Transplanting:**
- Take root cuttings or dig saplings
- Plant in moist soil (they love water)
- Space 6-10 feet apart (they get large)

**Harvest:** Berries in late summer (make syrup, wine, jam).

**Caution:** Raw berries can cause stomach upset—cook them first.

---

### 6. Wild Blackberries / Raspberries

**Transplanting:**
- Dig sucker shoots (new growth from roots)
- Plant in full sun, well-drained soil
- Space 2-3 feet apart

**Maintenance:** Prune annually, or they'll take over.

**Yield:** Starts producing in 1-2 years.

---

### 7. Wild Garlic / Onions

**Transplanting:**
- Dig bulbs in fall
- Replant immediately (don't let dry out)
- Space 4-6" apart

**Harvest:** Leaves in spring, bulbs in fall (leave some to multiply).

---

### 8. Watercress

**Where to find:** Clean, flowing streams.

**Transplanting:**
- Take stem cuttings
- Plant in shallow water or constantly moist soil
- **Must have flowing water or daily watering**

**Yield:** Rapid growth, cut-and-come-again green.

---

### 9. Wild Mint

**Transplanting:**
- Dig runners (underground stems)
- Plant anywhere (seriously, it grows everywhere)
- **Contain it** (raised bed or pot) or it will invade

**Uses:** Tea, cooking, insect repellent.

---

### 10. Jerusalem Artichoke (Sunchoke)

**Where to find:** Roadsides, old gardens (spreads like crazy).

**Transplanting:**
- Dig tubers in fall or spring
- Plant 4-6" deep, 12" apart

**Yield:** Massive (one tuber becomes dozens in one season).

**Caution:** Extremely invasive—plant only where you want it permanently.

---

## Propagation Methods

### Method 1: Root Division (Perennials)
1. Dig up established plant in fall or early spring
2. Separate into multiple sections (each with roots + shoots)
3. Replant immediately

**Best for:** Ramps, asparagus, nettle, mint.

---

### Method 2: Cuttings (Woody Plants)
1. Cut 6-12" section of stem (green or semi-hardwood)
2. Remove lower leaves
3. Dip in rooting hormone (optional—willow water works, see below)
4. Plant in moist soil
5. Keep humid (cover with plastic bag or jar)

**Best for:** Elderberry, willow, currants.

---

### Method 3: Seed Collection (See Section 5.6)
**Best for:** Dandelion, plantain, wild lettuce.

---

### Method 4: Transplanting Whole Plant
1. Dig wide around the root zone
2. Keep as much soil attached as possible
3. Replant immediately at the same depth
4. Water heavily for first 2 weeks

**Best for:** Small shrubs, young saplings.

---

## Willow Water (Natural Rooting Hormone)

**How to Make:**
1. Cut fresh willow twigs (any species)
2. Chop into 1-2" pieces
3. Soak in water for 24-48 hours
4. Strain out twigs

**How to Use:**
- Soak cuttings in willow water overnight before planting
- Water newly planted cuttings with willow water

**Why it works:** Willow contains salicylic acid (rooting hormone).

## Creating a Wild Food Forest

**Concept:** Layer wild edibles in vertical zones (canopy → ground cover).

### The 7 Layers
1. **Canopy:** Nut trees (oak, hickory—if you have space/time)
2. **Understory:** Elderberry, wild plum
3. **Shrubs:** Blackberries, raspberries
4. **Herbaceous:** Ramps, nettle, wild onions
5. **Ground cover:** Wild strawberries, wintergreen
6. **Root layer:** Jerusalem artichoke, wild carrots
7. **Vines:** Wild grapes (on fences/trees)

**Benefits:**
- Mimics natural ecosystems (low maintenance)
- Maximizes space (vertical production)
- Self-sustaining (perennials return every year)

---

## Encouraging Wild Growth (No Transplanting)

**Sometimes the best cultivation is just... letting them grow.**

### Identify and Protect Wild Patches
1. Find productive wild patches on your land (berry bushes, nut trees, asparagus)
2. Mark them (stakes, flags, GPS)
3. Protect from mowing, clearing, herbicides

### Improve Conditions
- **Clear competition:** Remove invasive weeds around desirable plants
- **Mulch:** Add organic matter (leaves, wood chips) around bases
- **Water during drought:** A little help = bigger yields

### Harvest Sustainably
- Take 1/3, leave 2/3 (Rule of thirds)
- Never over-harvest a patch (let it regenerate)

---

## Wildcrafting Ethics

**Don't:** 
- Take rare/endangered plants
- Strip a patch bare
- Trespass on private land

**Do:**
- Ask permission on private land
- Leave most of the plant/population
- Spread seeds to expand wild populations

---

## The 3-Year Wild Garden Plan

**Year 1: Scout and Transplant**
- Identify 5-10 wild edible species on your land or nearby
- Transplant 2-3 species to your garden

**Year 2: Expand and Propagate**
- Divide perennials from Year 1
- Add 3-5 new species

**Year 3: Harvest and Share**
- Begin harvesting established plants
- Trade divisions/cuttings with neighbors

**By Year 5:** You'll have a thriving wild food garden producing 100+ lbs of food annually with minimal effort.

---

## The Bottom Line

**Wild plants are pre-adapted survival crops.**

- They don't need pesticides
- They don't need fertilizer
- They don't need babysitting

**Your job:** Recognize them, transplant them, get out of their way.

**Start this spring:** Dig one clump of ramps, one nettle root, one asparagus crown.

**In 5 years, you'll have more food than you can eat—and you didn't buy a single seed.**
