# 18.1 Off-Grid Lighting

**The reality:** When the grid fails, darkness is absolute. No streetlights, no electricity, no light switches. Navigating your home becomes dangerous, and productive hours shrink to daylight only.

**This section covers sustainable, non-electric lighting—from candles to oil lamps.**

---

## The Lighting Hierarchy (By Efficiency)

| Method | Light Output | Fuel Cost | Burn Time | Ease |
|--------|-------------|-----------|-----------|------|
| **Daylight** | Excellent | Free | 10-14 hrs/day | NA |
| **Reflective windows** | Good (daytime) | Free | Passive | Easy |
| **Oil lamp** | Medium | Low | 8-12 hrs/fill | Medium |
| **Beeswax candle** | Low-Medium | Medium | 6-8 hrs | Medium |
| **Tallow candle** | Low | Low | 4-6 hrs | Easy |
| **Rushlight** | Very low | Very low | 1 hr per rush | Easy |

**Strategy:** Use free/passive during the day, conserve fuel at night.

---

## Maximizing Daylight (The Free Source)

### Window Placement and Reflection
**Even without electricity, you can extend usable daylight hours.**

**Techniques:**
1. **Keep windows clean:** Dirt reduces light by 30-40%
2. **Remove curtains during day:** Maximize light entry
3. **Reflective surfaces:** Hang mirrors or aluminum foil across from windows (doubles available light)
4. **Light-colored walls:** White/cream walls reflect 70-80% of light (vs. 10-15% for dark walls)

**Night prep:** Close curtains at dusk (retains heat, provides privacy).

## Candle Making (DIY Light Source)

### Material 1: Tallow (Animal Fat)

**Best for:** Budget lighting (uses waste fat from butchering).

#### Materials
- Rendered animal fat (beef, pork, sheep—see rendering process below)
- Wick material (cotton string, twisted cloth strips, or braided cotton)
- Mold (tin cans, cardboard milk cartons, or dip method)

#### Process: Rendering Tallow
1. Collect fat scraps from butchering (trim fat from meat)
2. Cut into small chunks (1" cubes)
3. Heat slowly in a pot over low fire (don't boil—you'll scorch it)
4. Liquid fat will separate from solids (called "cracklings")
5. Strain through cheesecloth into clean container
6. Let cool—tallow solidifies at room temp

**Yield:** 5 lbs of fat scraps = ~3 lbs of rendered tallow

---

#### Candle Method 1: Dipping (Traditional)
1. Tie wick to a stick (can dip multiple wicks at once)
2. Heat tallow until liquid (150-170°F)
3. Dip wick into tallow for 2-3 seconds
4. Pull out, let cool for 30 seconds
5. Repeat 20-30 times (each dip adds a thin layer)
6. Hang to cool completely

**Result:** Tapered candle (thick at base, thin at top).

**Time:** 30-60 minutes for a set of candles.

---

#### Candle Method 2: Molding (Faster)
1. Suspend wick in center of mold (tie to a stick laid across the top)
2. Pour melted tallow into mold
3. Let cool for 2-4 hours
4. Remove from mold (peel away if using cardboard/paper mold)

**Wick size:** Thicker candle = thicker wick (test and adjust).

---

### Material 2: Beeswax (Premium Quality)

**Advantages:** 
- Burns brighter and cleaner than tallow
- Pleasant honey scent
- Longer burn time

**Sources:**
- Harvest from your own beehives
- Trade with beekeepers
- Purchase (expensive, but worth it for special occasions)

**Process:** Same as tallow (dipping or molding), but beeswax melts at higher temp (145-150°F).

---

### Material 3: Paraffin Wax (If Available)

**Modern candle wax—byproduct of petroleum refining.**

**Where to get it:**
- Buy in bulk (craft stores)
- Melt down old candles

**Process:** Same as above (easiest to work with, melts at 130-150°F).

## Wick Selection (Critical)

**A bad wick = candle that won't stay lit or produces soot.**

### Best Wick Materials (Ranked)
1. **Braided cotton wick** (store-bought, ideal)
2. **Cotton string** (twisted tightly, 3 strands)
3. **Cloth strips** (torn from 100% cotton fabric, braided)
4. **Jute twine** (natural fiber, works but smoky)

**Wick thickness rule:**
- **1-inch diameter candle:** 3-strand cotton twist
- **2-inch diameter:** 6-strand or store-bought wick
- **3+ inch diameter:** Thick braided wick

**Test:** Light the candle. If flame is tiny and struggles, wick is too thin. If flame is huge and smoky, wick is too thick.

## Oil Lamps (Superior to Candles)

**Advantages over candles:**
- Brighter light
- Adjustable flame (turn wick up/down)
- Longer-lasting (8-12 hours per fill)
- Less messy

### DIY Oil Lamp (Simplest Design)

#### Materials
- Glass jar or bottle (any size)
- Metal lid with hole punched in center
- Wick (cotton string or cloth strip)
- Lamp oil or substitute (see fuels below)

#### Construction
1. Punch hole in lid (slightly smaller than wick diameter)
2. Thread wick through hole (leave 1/2" exposed above lid)
3. Fill jar 3/4 full with oil
4. Screw on lid (wick soaks up oil through capillary action)
5. Light the exposed wick

**That's it.** Ancient, simple, effective.

---

### Lamp Fuels (Ranked by Quality)

| Fuel | Light Output | Smoke | Smell | Cost | Notes |
|------|-------------|-------|-------|------|-------|
| **Lamp oil (kerosene)** | Excellent | Low | Minimal | Medium | Standard fuel |
| **Olive oil** | Good | Very low | Pleasant | High | Historical, clean-burning |
| **Vegetable oil** | Fair | Medium | Some | Low | Works, needs thick wick |
| **Rendered animal fat (liquid)** | Fair | High | Strong | Very low | Keep warm to stay liquid |

**Modern kerosene is best, but olive/vegetable oil works in a pinch.**

**Never use:** Gasoline (explosive), rubbing alcohol (burns too fast, dangerous).

---

### Multi-Wick Oil Lamp (Brighter)

**For more light output:**
- Use a wider jar
- Punch 3-5 holes in the lid
- Insert multiple wicks
- **Effect:** 3-5x the light of a single wick

## Rushlights (Ultra-Primitive, Ultra-Cheap)

**The poor man's candle**—used in medieval Europe.

### What is a rushlight?
A dried rush (cattail or bulrush) dipped in fat—burns for ~1 hour.

### How to Make
1. **Harvest rushes:** Cut green cattail or bulrush stems (2-3 feet long)
2. **Peel:** Remove outer green skin, leaving the white pith (spongy core)
3. **Dry:** Hang for 7-10 days until completely dry
4. **Dip in fat:** Melt tallow or grease, dip the rush (coat entire length except for 2-3 inches at one end—your "handle")
5. **Dry again:** Hang until fat solidifies

### How to Use
- Clip the rush at an angle in a holder (wooden clip, forked stick)
- Light one end
- As it burns, slowly tilt it upward (keeps the melting fat from dripping)

**Light output:** Dim (like a birthday candle).

**Best for:** Nighttime navigation (getting to the outhouse), not reading.

## Safety Protocols (Critical)

### Fire Hazards
- **Never leave candles/lamps unattended**
- **Keep away from flammable materials** (curtains, paper, bedding)
- **Use stable holders** (wide base, won't tip easily)
- **Extinguish before sleep** (or use a lantern with glass chimney)

### Carbon Monoxide Risk
**Burning anything indoors consumes oxygen and produces CO.**

- **Ventilate:** Crack a window when burning multiple candles/lamps
- **Watch for symptoms:** Headache, dizziness, nausea = too much CO
- **Limit indoor fires:** Don't burn 10 candles in a sealed room

### Burns
- **Hot wax/oil:** Melted wax can cause severe burns
- **Glass chimneys:** On oil lamps, these get extremely hot
- **Handle carefully:** Use cloth or gloves when moving lit lamps

## Lanterns (Wind-Proof Light)

**A lantern = oil lamp + protective enclosure.**

### DIY Tin Can Lantern
1. Punch holes in sides of tin can (ventilation + light dispersion)
2. Insert candle or small oil lamp inside
3. Add wire handle

**Advantages:**
- Wind-proof
- Safer (flame is enclosed)
- Portable

## Light Management Strategy (Conserving Fuel)

**In a long-term grid-down scenario, fuel is finite.**

### The 3-Light System
1. **Primary room light:** Oil lamp (kitchen, living area)
2. **Task light:** Single candle (reading, handwork)
3. **Navigation light:** Rushlight or small candle (moving between rooms)

**Don't light up the whole house—use only what you need.**

## Daily Lighting Schedule (Example)

| Time | Light Source | Activity |
|------|--------------|----------|
| **Dawn-10am** | Natural daylight | Morning chores, cooking |
| **10am-4pm** | Full daylight | Peak productivity (outdoor work, reading, sewing) |
| **4pm-sunset** | Reflected daylight | Prep for evening, gather firewood |
| **Sunset-9pm** | Oil lamp + candle | Cooking, eating, light tasks |
| **9pm-dawn** | Darkness (sleep) | Conserve fuel |

**The goal:** Align your schedule with natural light whenever possible.

## Long-Term Fuel Production

### Beeswax
- **1 hive** produces 1-2 lbs of wax per year
- **1 lb wax** = ~20 candles
- **Sustainable:** Harvest excess wax without harming the hive

### Tallow
- **1 butchered animal** (cow, pig) yields 10-30 lbs of fat
- **10 lbs fat** = ~7 lbs rendered tallow = ~70 candles
- **Renewable:** As long as you're raising livestock

### Oil Crops
- **Sunflower, flax, rapeseed:** Can be grown and pressed for oil
- **Labor-intensive:** Requires oil press
- **Yield:** Variable (depends on crop and acreage)

## The Emergency Lighting Kit

**If you can only stockpile/prepare 5 items:**

1. **50 lbs of tallow candles** (1 year of nightly use, 1 candle/night)
2. **5 gallons lamp oil (kerosene)** (1 year of nightly use, 1 lamp)
3. **10 cotton wicks** (replacements for oil lamp)
4. **2-3 glass oil lamps** (redundancy—one breaks, you have backup)
5. **Matches or lighter** (fire starting)

**Total cost:** ~$100-150  
**Total security:** 1 year of light

## The Bottom Line

**Light = safety, productivity, and morale.**

- Without light, you're confined to 8-10 productive hours per day
- With light, you extend to 14-16 hours
- **That's the difference between surviving and thriving.**

**Start now:**
- Make a batch of tallow candles (learn the skill)
- Build a simple oil lamp (test with vegetable oil)
- Stock 1 gallon of lamp oil (peace of mind)

**When the lights go out, you'll already know how to see in the dark.**
