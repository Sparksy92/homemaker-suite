# 12.2 Tree Knowledge

Trees aren't just lumber—they're medicine cabinets, food sources, and survival tools. These three species are widespread and offer the highest value-to-risk ratio for wildcrafting.

---

## 1. Pine (Pinus spp.)

**Species note:** Most pine species in North America are edible/medicinal. Avoid **Yew** (not a pine, needles are flat and toxic) and **Norfolk Island Pine** (houseplant, not a true pine).

### Identification

- **Needles:** Bundled in clusters (2, 3, or 5 needles per bundle)
- **Bark:** Reddish-brown, scaly, smells resinous
- **Cones:** Woody, with overlapping scales

**Key test:** Crush a needle—if it smells like Christmas tree/turpentine, it's a safe pine.

### Uses

#### Pine Needle Tea (Vitamin C Bomb)
**Why:** 5x more vitamin C than an orange (per volume). Essential for scurvy prevention.

**Preparation:**
1. Collect 1 cup of fresh green needles (any age)
2. Chop roughly (releases oils)
3. Pour boiling water over needles
4. Steep for 10 minutes (don't boil—destroys vitamin C)
5. Strain and drink

**Taste:** Citrusy, slightly resinous. Add honey if desired.

**Dosage:** 1-2 cups daily for immune support. Don't overdo it (high doses can irritate kidneys).

#### Pine Resin (Nature's Superglue)

**Harvesting:**
- Look for "pitch pockets" where bark is damaged
- Scrape off hardened resin with a knife
- Heat gently to make pliable

**Uses:**
- **Wound sealant:** Antimicrobial, waterproof (used historically for cuts)
- **Fire starter:** Ignites even when wet, burns hot
- **Glue:** Mix with charcoal powder (2:1 resin:charcoal) for strong adhesive

#### Inner Bark (Cambium Layer)

**Emergency Food:**
- Peel outer bark to reveal thin white/green layer underneath
- Scrape off and eat raw (tastes like mild cucumber) or dry into flour
- **Caution:** Only harvest from downed trees or dead branches (stripping live trees kills them)

## 2. Willow (Salix spp.)

**The original aspirin.** Every continent except Antarctica has willow species. Master this tree.

### Identification

- **Leaves:** Long, narrow, finely serrated edges
- **Growth:** Near water (streams, ponds, wetlands)
- **Bark:** Gray to brown, deeply furrowed on older trees
- **Twigs:** Flexible, don't snap easily (why they're used for baskets)

**Note:** Not all willows have "weeping" branches. Look for the narrow leaves and water proximity.

### Uses

#### Willow Bark Tea (Pain Relief)

**Why it works:** Contains **salicin**, which the body converts to salicylic acid (the active ingredient in aspirin).

**Preparation:**
1. Harvest young bark (green twigs, not old tree bark)
2. Scrape off outer bark, use the inner layer
3. Chop into small pieces (2 tbsp per cup of water)
4. Simmer gently for 20 minutes (don't boil hard—breaks down salicin)
5. Strain and sip slowly

**Dosage:**
- **Mild pain/fever:** 1 cup every 4-6 hours
- **Headache:** 1/2 cup, wait 30 minutes

**Caution:**
- Same precautions as aspirin (don't use if allergic to aspirin, avoid with blood thinners)
- Don't give to children under 12 (Reye's syndrome risk)
- Start with small amount to test tolerance

#### Willow for Containers & Cordage

**Basketry:**
- Cut long, flexible shoots in early spring
- Soak in water to keep pliable
- Weave into baskets (no tools needed beyond a knife)

**Lashing:**
- Strip bark into thin ribbons
- Use fresh (green) for flexibility
- Ties garden stakes, shelters, bundles

## 3. Maple (Acer spp.)

**The sugar tree.** Most famous for syrup, but offers more than just sweetness.

### Identification

- **Leaves:** Opposite branching (leaves grow in pairs across from each other)
- **Leaf shape:** Lobed (3-5 lobes, looks like the Canadian flag)
- **Seeds:** "Helicopters" (paired samaras that spin when falling)

**Common species:**
- **Sugar Maple (A. saccharum):** Best sap, 5-lobed leaves
- **Red Maple (A. rubrum):** Good sap, 3-lobed leaves, red stems
- **Silver Maple (A. saccharinum):** Watery sap, deeply cut leaves

### Uses

#### Maple Tapping (Spring Energy)

**When:** Late winter to early spring (freezing nights, thawing days)

**How to tap:**
1. Find a tree 10"+ diameter
2. Drill a hole 2-3 feet up, 1.5" deep, slight upward angle
3. Insert a spile (metal tap) or carved hollow elderberry twig
4. Hang bucket, cover with cloth (keeps debris out)

**Sap yield:**
- Each tap: 10-20 gallons per season
- **Don't over-tap:** 1 tap for 10-20" tree, 2 taps for 20"+

**Processing:**
- **Fresh sap:** Drink raw (tastes like slightly sweet water, 2% sugar)
- **Boiling to syrup:** 40:1 ratio (40 gallons sap = 1 gallon syrup)
- **Field method:** Boil in large pot over fire, skim foam, reduce until thick

**Tip:** If you can't boil it all, freeze the sap (lasts months).

#### Maple Seed "Helicopters" (Emergency Food)

**When:** Late spring to early summer

**Preparation:**
1. Remove the papery wing
2. Peel outer green coating (bitter)
3. Roast seeds in a pan until brown
4. Crack open, eat the nutmeat inside

**Taste:** Nutty, slightly sweet. Labor-intensive but calorie-dense.

#### Maple Leaves (Wound Poultice)

**Use:** Bruise fresh leaves (crush to release juices), apply to minor cuts (antimicrobial properties).

## Harvesting Ethics

**The 1/3 Rule:**
- Never take more than 1/3 of any plant/tree material in one harvest
- Leave enough for the tree to survive and for wildlife

**Live vs. Dead:**
- Prefer dead/downed trees for bark and inner cambium
- Only tap live maples if you'll monitor them (close holes after season)

**Avoid protected areas:**
- National/state parks often prohibit wildcrafting
- Always check local regulations
