# 16.2 Improvised Tools

**Reality Check:** Modern tools break. Stores close. Shipments stop. When that happens, you build your own or you go without.

This section covers **field-expedient tools**—functional implements made from scrap, natural materials, and improvisation.

---

## The Survival Toolbox: 5 Tools You Can Make

1. **Knife** (from scrap metal)
2. **Cordage** (from plant fibers)
3. **Containers** (from bark/wood)
4. **Fish hooks** (from wire, bone, thorns)
5. **Traps** (from wood and cordage)

---

## 1. Making a Functional Knife

### Materials Needed
- **Metal source:** Old lawn mower blade, car leaf spring, file, saw blade, or thick steel stock
- **Grinding tools:** File, angle grinder (if available), or abrasive stones
- **Handle material:** Wood, antler, cord-wrapped tang

### Process

#### Step 1: Shape the Blade
**If starting with scrap metal (lawn mower blade):**
1. Mark your blade shape with a marker or scribe (4-6" blade length is practical)
2. Cut out the rough shape:
   - **With grinder:** Cut along marked lines
   - **Without grinder:** Use a hacksaw (slow, but works)
3. Grind the bevel (15-20° edge angle)
   - Work from the spine toward the edge
   - Grind evenly on both sides
4. **Check for straightness:** Sight down the blade—should be perfectly straight

#### Step 2: Heat Treat (Hardening)
**This is critical—unhardened steel won't hold an edge.**

**What you need:**
- Fire or forge (wood fire works if hot enough)
- Oil for quenching (motor oil, vegetable oil, or lard)
- Magnet (to test temperature)

**Process:**
1. Heat blade to non-magnetic temperature:
   - Bury in hot coals (cherry red color, about 1400°F)
   - Test with magnet—when magnet no longer sticks, it's ready
2. **Quench immediately:** Plunge into oil, blade vertical (prevents warping)
3. **Test hardness:** Try to file the edge—file should skate across (not bite in)

**If you skip this step, your knife will bend instead of cutting.**

#### Step 3: Temper (Reduce Brittleness)
**Hardened steel is brittle—tempering makes it tough.**

1. Clean blade with sandpaper (removes oil)
2. Heat gently over coals until blade turns straw-yellow color (450°F)
3. Let cool slowly (air cool, don't quench)

**Why:** This reduces brittleness while keeping the edge hard.

#### Step 4: Add a Handle
**Option 1: Tang and wood scales**
1. Cut two pieces of wood to fit the tang (handle portion)
2. Sandwich the tang between the wood pieces
3. Drill holes through wood and tang
4. Rivet or bolt together (use nails if nothing else)

**Option 2: Cord wrap**
1. Wrap paracord or natural cordage tightly around the tang
2. Start at the guard, wrap toward the pommel
3. Tie off securely

#### Step 5: Sharpen
Use techniques from section 16.1 Tool Maintenance.

## 2. Natural Cordage (From Plants)

**Use:** Lashing, fishing line, bowstrings, nets, traps.

### Best Plant Fibers (by Strength)

| Plant | Strength | Processing | Availability |
|-------|----------|------------|--------------|
| **Nettle** | Excellent | Difficult (sting) | Common |
| **Dogbane** | Excellent | Moderate | Common (summer) |
| **Milkweed** | Good | Easy | Very common |
| **Yucca** | Excellent | Easy | Regional (arid areas) |
| **Cattail** | Fair | Easy | Wetlands |

### How to Make Cordage (Two-Ply Twist)

**Step 1: Harvest Fibers**
- **Nettle/dogbane:** Cut stalks, let dry 1-2 weeks, peel outer bark, strip inner fibers
- **Milkweed:** Same as above
- **Yucca:** Pull leaves, pound to separate fibers
- **Cattail:** Pull leaves, split into thin strips

**Step 2: Prepare Fibers**
1. Strip fibers to uniform thickness (1-2mm thick)
2. Keep them damp while working (spray with water or chew them—saliva works)

**Step 3: The Reverse-Wrap Twist**
**This is the universal cordage technique.**

1. Hold two fibers together, offset by a few inches
2. Twist the top fiber clockwise (between thumb and finger)
3. Wrap it counterclockwise over the other fiber
4. Now twist the new top fiber clockwise
5. Wrap counterclockwise
6. **Repeat forever**

**Key principle:** Twist one direction, wrap the opposite direction. This creates a self-locking cord.

**Adding length:**
- When a fiber is running out (2-3" left), add a new fiber
- Overlap the new fiber with the old by 3-4"
- Continue twisting and wrapping

**Result:** Strong, functional cordage. A 6-foot length takes ~30 minutes with practice.

### Testing Strength
- Cordage should support your body weight without snapping
- If it breaks easily, try:
  - Thicker fibers
  - Tighter twists
  - Better plant selection (nettle > cattail)

## 3. Bark Containers

**Use:** Carrying water, cooking (yes, over fire), storing food.

### Materials
- **Birch bark** (best—waterproof, flexible, peels in large sheets)
- **Cedar bark** (good, but requires more processing)
- **Spruce/pine bark** (usable, but brittle)

### Making a Birch Bark Container

**Step 1: Harvest Bark**
- Find a downed birch tree (don't strip live trees)
- Peel bark in the largest sheet possible (aim for 2' x 3')
- Warm it over a fire if it's brittle (makes it pliable)

**Step 2: Cut and Fold**
1. Cut a rectangular pattern:
   ```
   [--------10"---------]
   [    6"              ]
   ```
2. Mark fold lines 2" from each edge
3. Fold up the sides to create walls
4. Pinch corners and secure with:
   - Spruce root lacing (traditional)
   - Cordage stitching
   - Wooden pegs

**Step 3: Waterproof Seams (Optional)**
- Heat pine pitch (resin) until liquid
- Brush onto seams
- Let cool and harden

**Result:** Waterproof container that can be used over fire (bark doesn't burn if full of liquid).

## 4. Fish Hooks (Three Methods)

### Method 1: Wire Hook (Easiest)
**Materials:** Safety pin, paper clip, or any thin wire

**Process:**
1. Straighten the wire
2. Bend into a J-shape
3. File or grind a point on the short end (the barb)
4. Bend a tiny barb inward (prevents fish from escaping)
5. Tie to fishing line at the long end

**Test:** Should catch a fish's mouth when pulled.

---

### Method 2: Bone Hook (Traditional)
**Materials:** Chicken bone, rabbit leg bone, or any small animal bone

**Process:**
1. Cut a 1-2" section of bone (leg bones work best)
2. File one end to a sharp point
3. File a notch in the center (for tying line)
4. Optionally: carve a small barb near the point

**Tie:** Wrap line around the notch, secure with a tight knot.

**Advantage:** Won't rust, stronger than wire.

---

### Method 3: Thorn Hook (Ultra-Primitive)
**Materials:** Hawthorn, acacia, or honey locust thorns (1-2" long)

**Process:**
1. Select a thorn with a natural curve (or slightly bend it)
2. Lash to a small twig with cordage (creates the "shank")
3. Tie fishing line to the twig

**Limitation:** Small fish only (thorns break easily).

## 5. Survival Traps (No Tools Required)

### Deadfall Trap (Figure-4 Trigger)

**Use:** Small game (squirrels, rabbits)

**Materials:**
- 3 sticks (thickness of your thumb)
- Heavy rock or log (10-20 lbs)
- Bait

**How to Make:**

**The 3 Sticks:**
1. **Vertical stick:** Notched near the top
2. **Horizontal stick:** Pointed at one end, notched at the other
3. **Bait stick:** Notched to hook into the others

**Assembly:**
1. Carve notches so the sticks lock together in a "4" shape:
   ```
   [Rock balanced here]
        |  (vertical)
        |
   -----+  (horizontal, holds up vertical)
        |
     [Bait stick]
   ```
2. Balance the rock on top of the vertical stick
3. When an animal disturbs the bait stick, the whole structure collapses

**Safety:** Practice without the rock first. Deadfalls can injure you.

---

### Bottle Trap (For Fish)

**Materials:**
- Plastic bottle (2-liter soda bottle)
- Knife
- Cordage

**Process:**
1. Cut the bottle 1/3 from the top
2. Invert the top section, insert it into the bottom (creates a funnel)
3. Poke holes around the edge, tie the two sections together with cordage
4. Poke a few small holes in the bottom (lets water flow through)
5. Bait inside (bread, meat scraps)
6. Submerge in shallow water, weigh down with rocks

**How it works:** Fish swim in through the funnel, can't figure out how to swim back out.

**If you can only make ONE improvised tool:**
→ **Knife** (unlocks all other tool-making)

**If you can make TWO:**
→ Knife + **Cordage** (lashing, fishing, traps)

**If you can make THREE:**
→ Knife + Cordage + **Container** (water security)

**Everything else is a force multiplier, but these three are foundational.**

## 6. Stone Tools (Lithic Reduction)

**Use:** Cutting, scraping, arrowheads. The sharpest edge known to man (obsidian is sharper than surgical steel).

### Materials (The "Lithic" Triad)
1.  **Glassy Stone:** Flint, Chert, Obsidian, Quartzite, or thick plate glass (toilet tank lid/TV screen base).
2.  **Hammerstone:** A hard, round river rock (baseball size).
3.  **Pressure Flaker:** An antler tine or copper nail embedded in a stick.

### Technique 1: The Smash and Grab (Bipolar Percussion)
**For hard-to-work stones like Quartz.**
1.  Place target stone on a large "anvil" stone.
2.  Smash it with the hammerstone from above.
3.  **Result:** Sharp shards. Not pretty, but they cut meat.

### Technique 2: Spalling (Making a Flake)
1.  Find a stone with a flat surface (platform) and an angle less than 90°.
2.  Strike the edge of the platform sharply with the hammerstone.
3.  **Physics:** The force travels through the stone, popping off a "conchoidal fracture" (a sharp flake).
4.  **Use:** This flake is your knife. Use it as is, or shape it further.

### Technique 3: Pressure Flaking (Refining)
1.  Take your sharp flake.
2.  Place the edge against a leather pad in your palm.
3.  Press the **Pressure Flaker** (antler/copper) against the edge, pushing *in and down*.
4.  Small chips will pop off.
5.  Use this to shape the flake into an arrowhead or serrated knife.

## 7. Bone Tools

**Use:** Sewing, heavy piercing, fish gorges.

### Making an Awl (Leather Punch)
1.  **Select Bone:** Deer leg bone splinter (thick wall).
2.  **Abrade:** Rub against sandstone or concrete.
3.  **Shape:** Create a rounded handle end and a tapering sharp point.
4.  **Polish:** Rub with leather/sand to make it smooth (so it slides through hide).

### Making a Needle
1.  Start with a flat, thin bone splinter.
2.  Shape into a long, thin needle.
3.  **The Eye:**
    *   Use a sharp stone flake (drill) to bore a divot.
    *   Flip and bore from the other side until they meet.
    *   **Do not push through in one go** or you will split the bone.

## 8. Primitive Adhesives (Pine Pitch Glue)

**The "Superglue" of the woods.**

### The Formula
*   **Resin (Binder):** Pine/Spruce sap. Dried globs are best.
*   **Charcoal (Hardener):** Crushed into fine dust.
*   **Dung/Plant Fiber (Binder):** Dried rabbit/deer scat or cattail fluff.

### The Process
1.  **Melt Resin:** Put sap in a tin can or shell. Heat gently near fire (it is flammable!).
2.  **Filter:** Pour through grass to remove bugs/bark.
3.  **Add Charcoal:** Dimensions: **3 parts Resin : 1 part Charcoal dust**.
4.  **Add Fiber:** Pinch of crushed dung/fluff.
5.  **Mix:** Stir well with a stick.
6.  **Store:** Dip a stick into the mix, let it cool, dip again. Make a "glue stick."

### To Use
1.  Heat the glue stick over a flame until it drips.
2.  Apply to projectile point or tool handle.
3.  Smooth with a wet finger (lick your finger so it doesn't stick/burn).
4.  **Cures hard as epoxy in 5 minutes.**

## 9. Pottery (Container Revolution)

**Use:** Boiling water, cooking, storage (rodent proof).

### Step 1: Find Clay
*   Riverbanks: Look for slick, crackled mud.
*   Test: Roll a "snake" and wrap it around your finger. If it cracks, it's mud. If it holds, it's clay.

### Step 2: Processing (Temper)
*   Pure clay shrinks and cracks in fire.
*   **Add Temper:** 10-20% sand, crushed shell, or crushed fired pottery (grog).
*   Knead thoroughly to remove **all air bubbles** (air pockets = explosion in fire).

### Step 3: Forming (Coil Method)
1.  Make a flat pancake base.
2.  Roll long snakes of clay.
3.  Stack snakes in circles to build walls.
4.  **Smear:** Smooth the coils together (inside and out) so they become one solid wall.

### Step 4: Drying
*   Dry slowly in shade for 3-5 days.
*   If it feels cool to the cheek, it still has water. **Must be bone dry.**

### Step 5: Firing (Pit Fire)
1.  Dig a shallow pit.
2.  Place pots in center.
3.  Build a small fire *around* (not touching) them to warm them up (1 hour).
4.  Pile wood *over* them directly.
5.  Let burn hot for 2-3 hours.
6.  Let cool completely in ashes.

## The Skill Pyramid

**Making improvised tools isn't about memorizing steps—it's about:**
1.  **Observation:** Seeing materials as potential tools (that rock = hammer, that branch = handle).
2.  **Experimentation:** Trying, failing, adjusting.
3.  **Iteration:** Your first knife will be rough. Your tenth will be beautiful.

**Start now:** Don't wait for an emergency. Make a bark container on a Saturday. Twist cordage while watching TV. Build a deadfall in your backyard.

**When the grid fails, you won't rise to the occasion—you'll default to your training.**
