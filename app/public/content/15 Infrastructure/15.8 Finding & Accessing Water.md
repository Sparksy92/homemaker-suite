# 15.8 Finding & Accessing Water: The Deep Search

If you don't have a pre-drilled well, your survival depends on your ability to read the landscape and find water where others see only dirt and trees.

---

## 1. Indicator Plants (The Green Map)
Plants are the most reliable indicators of shallow groundwater. Look for these species, especially in dry seasons.

*   **Willows (Salix spp.):** Their roots must stay wet. If you see a willow, water is within 10-15 feet of the surface.
*   **Sycamore Trees:** Often follow the path of underground streams.
*   **Cattails & Sedges:** Indicate standing water or saturated soil just below the surface.
*   **Cottonwood Trees:** Huge water consumers. A lone cottonwood in a dry area is a "X marks the spot" for a well.

---

## 2. Reading Topography
*   **The V-Point:** Follow gullies or ravines down to where they converge. Even if the surface is dry, water often flows underneath.
*   **The Bench:** Look for a flat "bench" on a hillside where the vegetation suddenly becomes greener or thicker. This is often where an underground aquifer "leaks" out, forming a spring.
*   **Animal Trails:** Trails that converge like a funnel usually lead to a water source. Birds often fly in a straight line toward water at dusk.

---

## 3. Developing a Spring
A "seep" is water barely oozing from the ground. You can turn a seep into a reliable source.

### The Spring Box Method
1.  **Clean it out:** Dig back into the hillside where the water is oozing. Remove mud and rotting leaves until you find the "eye" of the spring (where the water comes from the rock or soil).
2.  **The Box:** Build a box (stone, concrete, or a food-grade plastic barrel) to catch the water.
3.  **Filtration:** Line the bottom of the box with 6 inches of clean gravel.
4.  **Overflow:** Ensure there is an overflow pipe so the water doesn't back up and find a new way out of the hill (which would "kill" your spring).

---

## 4. Digging a Seep Well (The Indian Well)
Used when you are near a dry creek bed or in a low-lying area.

1.  **Location:** Find the outside bend of a dry creek bed (water stays here longer).
2.  **Dig:** Dig a hole 3-5 feet deep.
3.  **Wait:** Let the water seep in from the surrounding soil. It will be muddy at first.
4.  **Line it:** Use large stones to line the walls of the hole to prevent collapse.
5.  **Cover it:** Put a lid over it to prevent animals from falling in and drowning (which ruins the water).

---

## 5. Driven Point Wells (The "Sand Point")
If your soil is sandy or gravelly (no big rocks), you can "drive" a well by hand.

*   **Tools:** A "Well Point" (a perforated pipe with a steel tip) and lengths of 1.25" galvanized pipe.
*   **Method:** Use a heavy post-driver or sledgehammer to drive the pipe into the ground. Add 5-foot sections of pipe as you go.
*   **Testing:** Drop a weighted string down the pipe. If it comes up wet, you've hit the water table.
*   **Pump:** Attach a manual pitcher pump to the top.

---

## 6. Solar Water Stills (Last Resort)
If the ground is damp but you can't find a flow.

1.  Dig a hole 3 feet wide, 2 feet deep.
2.  Place a collection jar in the center.
3.  Cover the hole with a clear plastic sheet, weighted at the edges.
4.  Place a small stone in the center of the plastic so it dips down over the jar.
5.  The sun evaporates moisture from the soil; it condenses on the plastic and drips into the jar.

---

## Safety Warning
**Surface water and shallow wells are easily contaminated.**
*   Always test for E. coli and nitrates once a year.
*   Never dig a well within 100 feet of your latrine or humanure pile.
*   **All** water from these sources must be filtered or boiled before drinking.
