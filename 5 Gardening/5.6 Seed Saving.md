# 5.6 Seed Saving

**The Ultimate Food Security:** A single tomato contains 100+ seeds. One packet of saved seeds = next year's garden. Break the cycle of dependence on seed companies.

**This is generational wealth—knowledge + seeds that multiply forever.**

---

## Why Save Seeds?

1. **Independence:** Never buy seeds again
2. **Adaptation:** Seeds adapt to your specific climate/soil over generations
3. **Variety preservation:** Heirloom varieties disappear when no one saves them
4. **Barter value:** Seeds are lightweight, valuable trade goods
5. **Insurance:** Crop failure? You have backup seeds for next season

---

## The Seed-Saving Hierarchy (Start Here)

**Easiest (Beginners):**
1. **Tomatoes** (wet processing, self-pollinating)
2. **Beans** (dry, shell, store—that's it)
3. **Peas** (same as beans)
4. **Lettuce** (let bolt, collect dry seeds)

**Moderate:**
5. **Peppers** (self-pollinating, easy to dry)
6. **Cucumbers/squash** (need isolation to prevent cross-pollination)
7. **Herbs** (varied methods)

**Advanced:**
8. **Corn** (wind-pollinated, needs large population)
9. **Carrots/beets** (biennial—takes 2 years)
10. **Brassicas** (cabbage, broccoli—biennial, cross-pollinate easily)

**Start with tomatoes and beans. Master those, then expand.**

## Seed Saving Rule #1: Open-Pollinated vs. Hybrid

### Open-Pollinated (OP) / Heirloom
- **Save these:** Seeds grow "true to type" (offspring = parent plant)
- **Examples:** Most heirloom tomatoes, beans, peas

### Hybrid (F1)
- **Don't save these:** Seeds won't match the parent (genetics are unstable)
- **How to identify:** Seed packet says "F1" or "hybrid"
- **Examples:** Most modern tomatoes, corn, squash from big seed companies

**Check your seed packets.** If it doesn't say "hybrid," it's probably open-pollinated.

## Tomato Seed Saving (Wet Processing)

**Best for:** Tomatoes, cucumbers, melons (seeds embedded in wet flesh).

### Process

#### Step 1: Select the Best Fruit
- Choose from the healthiest, most productive plant
- Let the fruit **fully ripen** on the plant (even slightly overripe is fine)

#### Step 2: Extract Seeds
1. Cut tomato in half
2. Squeeze seeds and gel into a glass or jar
3. Add a small amount of water (1/4 cup)

#### Step 3: Ferment (Critical Step)
1. Leave jar at room temp for 2-4 days (covered loosely)
2. Stir once daily
3. **Why ferment?** Removes gel coating, kills seed-borne diseases, improves germination

**Signs of proper fermentation:**
- Mold forms on surface (this is normal—don't panic)
- Smells sour/funky
- Gel coating dissolves

#### Step 4: Rinse and Dry
1. Add more water, stir vigorously
2. Good seeds sink, bad seeds/pulp floats
3. Pour off floating debris
4. Rinse seeds in a fine strainer under running water
5. Spread on a plate or screen to dry (7-10 days)

**Test dryness:** Snap a seed in half—should break cleanly (not bend).

#### Step 5: Store
- Paper envelope or glass jar (labeled with variety and year)
- Store in cool, dry, dark place

**Shelf life:** 4-10 years (tomato seeds are long-lived).

## Bean & Pea Seed Saving (Dry Processing)

**Easiest method.**

### Process

#### Step 1: Let Pods Dry on the Plant
- Don't harvest beans/peas for eating
- Leave pods on the plant until completely dry and brown
- Pods should rattle when shaken (seeds loose inside)

#### Step 2: Harvest Pods
- Pick pods on a dry day (moisture = mold)
- If rain is coming, harvest early and dry indoors

#### Step 3: Shell
- Crack open pods, remove seeds
- Discard shriveled or damaged seeds

#### Step 4: Dry Further (If Needed)
- Spread seeds on a screen for 1-2 weeks (ensures complete dryness)

#### Step 5: Store
- Glass jar or sealed container
- Cool, dark, dry location

**Shelf life:** 2-5 years.

---

## Pepper Seed Saving

**Similar to tomatoes, but no fermentation.**

### Process
1. Select fully ripe pepper (red, yellow, or orange—not green)
2. Cut open, scrape out seeds
3. Spread on plate to dry (1-2 weeks)
4. Store

**Cross-pollination note:** Peppers can cross-pollinate. If saving multiple varieties, separate by 50+ feet or hand-pollinate (cover flowers with bags).

---

## Lettuce Seed Saving

**Let it bolt!** (Go to seed.)

### Process
1. Let 2-3 lettuce plants grow past the eating stage
2. Plants will send up tall flower stalks (3-4 feet)
3. Flowers turn into fluffy seed heads (like tiny dandelions)
4. When seed heads are dry and fluffy, harvest

**Harvesting:**
- Bend stem over a paper bag
- Shake or rub seed heads—seeds fall into bag
- **Yield:** One plant = hundreds of seeds

**Storage:** Cool, dry place (2-5 year shelf life).

---

## Squash & Cucumber Seed Saving

**Challenge:** Cross-pollination (different varieties will interbreed).

### Isolation Methods

**Option 1: Distance**
- Plant only one variety of squash/cucumber
- OR separate varieties by 1/2 mile+ (for absolute purity)

**Option 2: Hand-Pollination**
1. Identify male flowers (thin stem) vs. female flowers (swollen base = baby squash)
2. Tape female flowers closed in the evening (before they open)
3. In the morning, pick a male flower, remove petals
4. Rub pollen onto female flower
5. Tape female flower closed again (prevents bees from cross-pollinating)

**This guarantees pure seeds but is labor-intensive.**

### Seed Extraction
1. Let squash fully ripen (even past eating stage—skin should be hard)
2. Cut open, scoop out seeds
3. Rinse off flesh
4. Dry on screen (7-14 days)
5. Store

**Shelf life:** 4-6 years.

---

## Herb Seed Saving

### Cilantro/Coriander
- Let plants bolt and flower
- Seeds turn brown (these are coriander seeds)
- Harvest, dry, store

### Dill
- Same as cilantro (let flower, harvest dry seeds)

### Basil
- Let flower stalks dry on plant
- Rub dried flowers—tiny black seeds fall out
- Store

## Biennial Crops (2-Year Process)

**Plants that flower and seed in their second year:** Carrots, beets, cabbage, onions, parsley.

### Process
1. **Year 1:** Grow plant normally, harvest some for eating
2. **Fall:** Dig up best roots (carrots, beets) or leave in ground (if mild winter)
3. **Winter:** Store roots in cold, moist conditions (root cellar) OR mulch heavily in the ground
4. **Year 2:** Replant in spring (or leave if overwintered in place)
5. Plant sends up flower stalk, produces seeds
6. Harvest seeds when dry

**This requires patience and space**—not for beginners.

## Seed Storage Tips

### The 3 Enemies of Seed Longevity
1. **Moisture** (causes mold and premature sprouting)
2. **Heat** (degrades seed viability)
3. **Light** (breaks down seed chemistry)

### Ideal Storage Conditions
- **Cool:** 32-50°F (refrigerator is ideal, but not freezer)
- **Dry:** <10% moisture (use silica gel packets if in humid climates)
- **Dark:** Opaque containers or dark location

### Containers (Ranked)
1. **Glass jars with tight lids** (best—airtight, reusable)
2. **Paper envelopes inside glass jars** (keeps varieties separate)
3. **Plastic baggies** (okay, but not long-term—moisture can penetrate)

**Label everything:** Variety name, date saved, any special notes (e.g., "best producer").

## Seed Viability Testing

**If seeds are old, test before planting your whole garden.**

### The Paper Towel Test
1. Dampen paper towel, fold in half
2. Place 10 seeds on towel
3. Roll up towel, place in plastic bag (keeps moist)
4. Wait 7-14 days
5. Count how many sprouted

**Results:**
- 8-10 sprouted = excellent (80-100% viability)
- 5-7 sprouted = good (50-70%—plant extra to compensate)
- 0-4 sprouted = poor (get fresh seeds)

## Building a Seed Library

**Goal:** 3-year supply of seeds for every crop you grow.

**Why 3 years?**
- Year 1: Plant and grow
- Year 2: Backup (in case Year 1 fails)
- Year 3: Insurance (in case Years 1-2 both fail)

**Example:**
- Tomatoes: Save seeds from 5-10 plants → ~500-1000 seeds → enough for 10+ years

## The Exchange Economy

**Seeds are currency in a grid-down world.**

**What to trade:**
- Excess seeds (you saved 500, you only need 50)
- Rare heirloom varieties
- Pre-packaged seed packets (labeled, dated)

**What to request:**
- Varieties you don't have
- Fresh seeds (to replace old stock)
- Knowledge (seed-saving techniques from experienced gardeners)

---

## Seed Security Strategy

**Don't put all your seeds in one basket.**

**Distribute storage:**
1. **Primary stash:** At home (cool, dry place)
2. **Backup stash:** Friend's house or off-site location
3. **Trading stash:** Small packets ready to barter

**Why?** Fire, flood, or theft won't wipe out your entire seed bank.
