# 17.1 Emergency Shelters

**The Rule of Threes:** You can survive 3 minutes without air, 3 hours without shelter (in harsh conditions), 3 days without water, 3 weeks without food.

**Shelter is second only to breathing.**

---

## Shelter Priorities

1. **Location** (wrong spot = failed shelter, no matter how well-built)
2. **Insulation** (from ground and air)
3. **Weatherproofing** (wind, rain, snow)
4. **Size** (smaller = warmer)

**Speed matters:** Building a rough shelter in 1 hour beats building a perfect shelter in 4 hours when you're hypothermic.

---

## Site Selection (Critical)

### The Ideal Site Checklist
✅ **Flat or slightly sloped** (water drains away, not into your shelter)  
✅ **Dry** (no standing water, not in a drainage path)  
✅ **Wind protection** (behind a hill, rock outcrop, or dense trees)  
✅ **Materials nearby** (branches, leaves, bark within 50 feet)  
✅ **No dead trees overhead** (widow-makers can kill you)  
✅ **Away from water** (200+ feet to avoid insects, flooding, and cold air)

### Sites to AVOID
❌ **Valley bottoms** (cold air pools here at night)  
❌ **Ridgelines** (exposed to wind)  
❌ **Under dead limbs** (can fall without warning)  
❌ **Near water** (mosquitoes, flooding, colder temps)  
❌ **Animal trails** (disturbed sleep, potential conflict)

## Shelter Type 1: Debris Hut (Solo, 3-Season)

**Best for:** Overnight emergency, solo survival, no tools.

**Pros:** Fast (1-2 hours), warm, uses only natural materials  
**Cons:** Cramped, not weatherproof in heavy rain

### Materials Needed
- Ridgepole (straight branch, 8-10 feet long, thick as your arm)
- Ribs (20-30 branches, 4-6 feet long)
- Debris (leaves, pine needles, grass—armload upon armload)

### Construction

#### Step 1: Build the Frame
1. Find or create an anchor point:
   - **Option A:** Lean ridgepole against a tree stump or low fork
   - **Option B:** Prop ridgepole between two trees
   - **Option C:** Create an A-frame with stacked rocks
2. The ridgepole should be 3-4 feet off the ground at the high end, touching the ground at the low end

#### Step 2: Add Ribs
1. Lean branches along both sides of the ridgepole (like ribs on a spine)
2. Space them 6-12 inches apart
3. Angle: 45-60° (steep enough to shed rain)

#### Step 3: Layer Debris (The Insulation)
**This is the critical step—most people don't use enough.**

1. Pile leaves/debris on the frame until it's **2-3 feet thick**
2. Start at the bottom, work your way up (like shingling a roof)
3. Pack it down, add more (it will compress)

**Rule of thumb:** If you can see through the walls, you need more debris.

#### Step 4: Create a Door
1. Stuff the entrance with a pile of leaves (your "door")
2. Crawl in feet-first, pull the leaf pile in behind you

#### Step 5: Insulate the Floor
1. Before entering, pile 6-12 inches of dry leaves/pine needles inside
2. This is **critical**—ground sucks heat faster than air

### Testing
- Crawl inside (you should barely fit—tight = warm)
- If you can feel wind, add more debris
- If you can see light through the walls, add more debris

**In freezing weather, a proper debris hut keeps you alive.** It won't be comfortable, but it works.

## Shelter Type 2: Lean-To (Group, Temporary)

**Best for:** 2-4 people, short-term (1-3 days), when you have basic tools.

**Pros:** Spacious, can build a fire in front, easier to enter/exit  
**Cons:** Less insulation than debris hut, requires more materials

### Materials Needed
- 2 upright posts (5-6 feet tall, thick as your wrist)
- 1 horizontal ridgepole (10-12 feet long)
- 10-15 support poles (angled from ridgepole to ground)
- Thatching material (branches, bark, leaves)
- Cordage (natural or improvised)

### Construction

#### Step 1: Build the Frame
1. Drive or wedge two upright posts into the ground (8-10 feet apart)
2. Lash the ridgepole across the tops of the posts (4-5 feet high)

#### Step 2: Add Support Poles
1. Lean poles from the ridgepole to the ground at a 45-60° angle
2. Space 12-18 inches apart
3. Lash to the ridgepole if you have cordage

#### Step 3: Thatch the Roof
**Layer from bottom to top (like roofing shingles):**
1. Lay branches across the support poles
2. Cover with bark sheets (birch, cedar) if available
3. Top with layers of leaves, grass, or evergreen boughs
4. **Thickness:** 12-18 inches minimum

#### Step 4: Build a Reflector Wall (Optional, but Recommended)
**This makes a huge difference for heat retention:**
1. Build a wall of logs or rocks 3-4 feet from the shelter opening
2. Build your fire between the wall and the shelter
3. The reflector bounces heat into the lean-to

### Fire Safety
- Keep fire 4-6 feet from shelter entrance
- Never build fire INSIDE a thatched shelter (it will catch fire)
- Clear all flammable material from around the fire pit

## Shelter Type 3: Snow Shelter (Quinzhee)

**Best for:** Winter survival, when snow is plentiful (2+ feet deep).

**Pros:** Excellent insulation (inside stays 20-40°F warmer than outside), protects from wind  
**Cons:** Requires significant effort, takes 3-4 hours

### Construction

#### Step 1: Pile Snow
1. Shovel or pile snow into a dome (8-10 feet diameter, 5-6 feet tall)
2. Pack it down as you build
3. Let it sit for 1-2 hours (snow crystals bond together—this is critical)

#### Step 2: Insert Guide Sticks
1. Push 12-inch sticks into the dome from all directions
2. These show you when walls are 12 inches thick (safe thickness)

#### Step 3: Hollow Out the Interior
1. Dig an entrance (2-3 feet wide, on the downwind side)
2. Dig upward and inward (creates a "cold well" at the entrance)
3. Hollow out the interior until you hit the guide sticks
4. **Don't make walls thinner than 12 inches** (collapse risk)

#### Step 4: Ventilation (Critical)
1. Poke a fist-sized hole in the roof (prevents CO2 buildup)
2. Keep this hole clear of ice

#### Step 5: Insulate the Floor
- Lay evergreen boughs, backpack, or sleeping pad on the floor
- **Do not sleep directly on snow** (heat loss)

### Temperature Inside
- Outside: 0°F → Inside: 20-35°F (before body heat)
- With your body heat: 35-45°F

**A quinzhee can mean the difference between frostbite and comfort.**

## Shelter Type 4: Tarp Shelter (If You Have a Tarp)

**Best for:** Fast setup, versatility, when you have modern materials.

### The A-Frame Tarp Shelter

**Materials:**
- 8x10 tarp (or larger)
- 50 feet of cordage
- 2 trees or trekking poles

**Setup (5 minutes):**
1. String a ridgeline between two trees (5-6 feet high)
2. Drape tarp over ridgeline
3. Stake out corners at 45° angle
4. Adjust tension (tight = better rain shedding)

**Pros:** Fastest shelter, weatherproof, packable  
**Cons:** Requires carrying a tarp

---

## Ground Insulation (The Forgotten Essential)

**Heat loss to the ground is faster than heat loss to air.** A sleeping bag without ground insulation loses 50%+ of its effectiveness.

### Improvised Ground Pads

1. **Evergreen boughs:** Layer 6-12 inches thick, needles facing up
2. **Dry leaves:** 12-18 inches thick (compresses to ~6")
3. **Bark slabs:** Flat pieces stacked 2-3 layers
4. **Grass/hay:** Thick bundles, woven or piled

**Even improvised insulation beats sleeping on bare ground.**

---

## Multi-Day Shelter Improvements

**If you're stuck for 3+ days, upgrade your shelter:**

1. **Reinforce the frame:** Add more support poles
2. **Thicken the walls:** Double the debris/thatching
3. **Dig a drainage trench:** 6 inches deep, around the perimeter (diverts rain)
4. **Create a windbreak:** Pile branches or build a wall upwind
5. **Improve the door:** Weave a mat from branches/grass

**A well-built debris hut can last weeks.**

---

## Shelter vs. Fire (The Trade-Off)

**In survival scenarios, you often face a choice:**
- Spend 3 hours building a great shelter (no fire needed)
- Spend 1 hour building a rough shelter + maintain a fire all night

**Best approach:** 
1. Build the best shelter you can in 90 minutes
2. Use remaining daylight to gather firewood
3. Fire = backup heat if shelter isn't warm enough

**Never rely on fire alone.** Fire goes out. Shelter stays.

---

## The Overnight Test

**Before committing to sleep in a shelter, test it:**
1. Crawl inside for 30 minutes
2. Can you feel wind? (Add more insulation)
3. Is the ground cold? (Add more floor insulation)
4. Are you cramped? (Smaller = warmer in winter; larger = cooler in summer)

**Fix problems before dark.**

---

## Emergency Shelter Hierarchy (By Speed)

| Shelter | Build Time | Warmth | Weather Protection |
|---------|------------|--------|-------------------|
| **Tarp A-frame** | 5 min | Low | Excellent |
| **Lean-to** | 1-2 hrs | Medium | Good |
| **Debris hut** | 1-2 hrs | Excellent | Fair |
| **Quinzhee** | 3-4 hrs | Excellent | Excellent |

**Choose based on your situation:**
- **Hypothermic now:** Tarp or quick lean-to + fire
- **Need overnight warmth:** Debris hut
- **Winter survival:** Quinzhee

**The best shelter is the one you can finish before you freeze.**

---

## The Ultimate Rule

**Your shelter exists to keep you alive, not comfortable.**

- Tight fit = warm
- Ugly = functional
- Fast = better than perfect

**Build it. Test it. Sleep in it. Live.**
